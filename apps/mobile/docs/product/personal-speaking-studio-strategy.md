# Personal Speaking Studio Strategy

> Status: Current product and go-to-market direction
>
> Recorded: 2026-08-16
>
> Scope: English-first global product; behavior-based initial audience
>
> Working-name note: `Saylo` is not treated as a cleared public brand name in this document.

## Executive definition

**영어를 이미 어느 정도 아는 사람이, 콘텐츠와 일상에서 발견한 표현을 자기
Topic 안에서 직접 말해 보고, 거울 앞에서 연습하고, 필요할 때 다시 꺼내 쓰게
하는 개인 speaking studio.**

Consumer promise:

> **The English you save should come back when you speak.**

Supporting line:

> Turn the English you collect into English you can use.

The product is not a beginner curriculum, a generic AI conversation partner, or a
workplace-performance coach. It is a personal activation layer between the English a
learner encounters and the English that becomes available in their own speech.

## Strategic thesis

The product is built around one persistent gap:

```text
English I encounter and understand
              ↓
English I save and review
              ↓
English I can retrieve for my own meaning
              ↓
English that has become part of my voice
```

Most products own one portion of this path:

- curriculum and AI tutors provide language and guided practice;
- immersion tools help learners understand and capture language from real content;
- flashcards strengthen recognition and recall;
- speech coaches evaluate delivery in a scenario;
- voice notes record speech without building a learning system around it.

This product should own the transition from **saved language to personally usable
spoken language**.

## Market judgment and whitespace

### 1. Curriculum and AI-tutor products

Speak and Praktika already provide speaking-first lessons, AI conversation, custom
topics, corrections, personalized plans, and review. Speak explicitly positions its
method as `Learn → Practice → Apply` and says it moves passive knowledge into active
spoken fluency. Praktika offers free conversation on any topic and contextual
suggestions.

These products prove demand for spoken practice, but they also mean that the promise
"turn passive English into active English" is not differentiated on its own.

- [Speak product and method](https://www.speak.com/)
- [Praktika product](https://praktika.ai/)

### 2. Immersion, sentence-mining, and capture tools

Language Reactor, LingQ, and Migaku demonstrate a large global population of
self-directed learners who prefer real content to a fixed beginner curriculum.

- Language Reactor reports 2,000,000 Chrome users and focuses on understanding
  Netflix, YouTube, books, and websites.
- LingQ reports more than 5,000,000 learners and centers real-content import,
  vocabulary growth, and comprehension.
- Migaku's public flow turns streaming, websites, and social media into interactive
  material and spaced-repetition cards; its Chrome extension has tens of thousands
  of users.

These counts overlap, include multiple target languages, and are not a market-size
sum. They are evidence that real-content and sentence-mining behavior exists at
meaningful global scale.

- [Language Reactor Chrome listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/language-reactor/hoombieeljmmljlkjmnheibnpciblicm)
- [LingQ](https://www.lingq.com/en/)
- [Migaku Chrome listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/migaku-really-learn-langu/lkhiljgmbeecmljiogckofcalncmfnfo?hl=en)

### 3. Workplace and performance coaches

WorkVoice, Yoodli, and Talaera's Tally optimize interviews, meetings,
presentations, sales calls, delivery, or professional performance. They validate
high-stakes speaking needs, but that category is narrower and more corporate than
the intended identity here.

This product may acquire users through an interview, pitch, presentation, or meetup,
but those moments are **campaigns and Topic templates**, not the product category.

- [WorkVoice](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/workvoice-ai-meeting-coach/id6766565386)
- [Yoodli overview](https://support.yoodli.ai/en/articles/9550461-yoodli-overview)
- [Talaera Tally](https://www.talaera.com/ai-communication-coach-talk-to-tally/)

### 4. Emerging direct competitors

Smaller products such as ConvoCopilot already combine personal phrase libraries,
spaced repetition, and AI conversation. The feature combination is therefore not
an empty category or a durable moat by itself.

- [ConvoCopilot App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/convocopilot/id6759226184)

The defensible opening must be narrower:

```text
save from anywhere
→ attach to a durable personal Topic
→ self-talk without an AI conversation partner
→ analyze intended meaning and actual retrieval
→ recommend one small repair
→ speak again
→ preserve evidence that the phrase returned in the learner's own speech
```

### The whitespace statement

> Other products help learners find English, remember English, talk to an AI, or
> perform in a work scenario. This product helps English found anywhere become part
> of the learner's own voice inside the Topics that make up their life.

## The problem and supporting evidence

### Receptive knowledge is not productive access

Learners can recognize a word or expression in listening and reading without being
able to produce it in speech. A 2024 study of 205 adult English learners found that
receptive and productive collocation knowledge behave as distinct constructs.

- [Receptive and productive L2 collocation study](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijal.12605)

A smaller retrieval-practice study found productive retrieval more effective than
receptive retrieval or repetition on a productive vocabulary test. This supports
the mechanism, but its small sample does not prove that this product will improve
speaking.

- [Productive retrieval study](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/katejournal/30/0/30_11/_article)

### The four user pains

1. **Collector's gap**
   The learner saves expressions in screenshots, Notes, Anki, bookmarks, or a
   content-learning tool, but rarely uses them in spontaneous speech.

2. **Safe-language loop**
   When speaking, the learner falls back to familiar, simple expressions even when
   they understand richer language.

3. **Topic paralysis**
   Open-ended AI conversation creates another task: deciding what to talk about.
   Random prompts do not accumulate into a personal body of language.

4. **Practice friction**
   Capturing, organizing, reviewing, speaking, and evaluating language across
   several tools requires too many decisions and steps.

Self-talk and Mirror mode are the proposed delivery mechanism. Productive retrieval
research supports speaking from meaning rather than merely recognizing language,
but the specific value of Mirror mode, Topic persistence, and the product's AI
analysis must be validated with real users.

## Target audience

### The product is global, but not for everyone

The product is English-first and global. It does **not** select a primary market by
the learner's first language or nationality.

The initial audience is defined by current behavior, proficiency, and motivation:

- approximately B1 to C1 English;
- consumes English video, podcasts, articles, shows, or social content at least
  several times per week;
- already saves or notices useful expressions;
- can hold a basic conversation but reuses safe language and struggles to retrieve
  richer expressions;
- wants to talk about personal interests, life, study, ideas, projects, or work;
- is comfortable practicing privately through self-talk or is willing to try it;
- does not want to restart a beginner curriculum;
- values their own meaning and voice over a polished AI-written script.

Primary user statement:

> I understand and save a lot of English, but when I speak, the expressions I wanted
> to use disappear.

### Initial global distribution clusters

The audience remains behavioral, while distribution begins with a small number of
language-community clusters where the founder has credible access.

Initial clusters may include:

- followers of Chinese-speaking creators who teach intermediate English methods;
- Spanish-speaking communities discussing the intermediate plateau, immersion, or
  sentence mining;
- Russian-speaking communities discussing active vocabulary and speaking practice;
- multilingual international-student, creator, builder, and self-directed learner
  communities;
- viewers of English-learning creators whose content is explicitly for
  intermediate or advanced learners rather than beginners.

The creators' language is a distribution channel, not a product segmentation rule.
All cohorts should enter the same English-first product and be measured by the same
behavioral activation definition.

### Initial non-users

- A0–A2 learners who need foundational instruction;
- test-only learners seeking a score curriculum;
- users focused only on accent elimination or pronunciation scoring;
- users who want AI to write a complete answer for them;
- passive content viewers who do not save language and have no interest in speaking;
- users seeking a human tutor or an AI avatar as the central experience.

## Product identity

Different audiences need different category language. One phrase does not need to
serve every purpose.

| Purpose | Category language |
| --- | --- |
| App Store and search | English speaking practice for intermediate learners |
| Consumer brand | Personal speaking studio |
| Product mechanism | Topic-based self-talk |
| Technical and investor narrative | Personal language activation layer / Speaking Memory |
| Signature experience | Mirror mode + private voice history |
| Future network layer | Optional Audience Check |

### Brand promise

> The English you save should come back when you speak.

### Supporting promise

> Bring a phrase from a show, podcast, article, or real conversation. Add it to a
> Topic that matters to you, rehearse it in Mirror mode, and make it part of how you
> speak.

### Brand principles

1. **Your voice first.** The learner supplies the meaning, facts, opinions, and life.
2. **Topics over curriculum.** The learner's life structures practice.
3. **Retrieval over collection.** Saving is history; returning in speech is progress.
4. **Progress you can hear.** Compare attempts instead of manufacturing a global
   fluency score.
5. **Private by default.** Personal audio and Topics are not social content unless
   the learner explicitly chooses otherwise.
6. **One useful next step.** AI should reduce correction overload.

### Language to use

- your Topic
- something you want to say
- a phrase you found
- your voice
- try again
- keep this
- hear what changed
- ready when you need it
- saved → spoken

### Language to avoid

- speak like a native
- fix your accent
- master English
- workplace English coach as the top-level category
- AI tutor as the top-level category
- performance score
- fluent fast
- anonymous voice

Work, pitch, and interview Topics remain useful, but they should not dominate the

## Core product loop

```text
1. Find a useful expression in content or life
2. Save it in under 10–20 seconds with source and minimal context
3. Attach it to an existing Topic or create one
4. Speak about the Topic for 30–90 seconds without seeing the target expression
5. AI identifies intended meaning, one gap, and whether saved language returned
6. AI offers at most 1–3 relevant expressions with provenance
7. Complete one immediate second attempt
8. Preserve Recognize → Retrieve → Use evidence
9. Prompt the expression again inside a future Topic or Version
```

The durable object model remains:

```text
Speaking World
└── Topic
    ├── Version
    ├── Useful language with provenance
    └── Attempt history
```

## AI capability focus

The differentiator is not the presence of a general-purpose model. It is the
quality and restraint of the post-self-talk analysis.

After an attempt, AI should answer only five questions well:

1. What was the learner trying to mean?
2. Where did that meaning become vague, incomplete, or difficult to retrieve?
3. Which saved expression was actually retrieved or used?
4. Which 1–3 learner-relevant expressions best support the next attempt?
5. What is the single highest-value next action?

Example output:

```text
What you meant
You started this project because the existing tools felt too complicated.

What got vague
You described the tool, but not the moment that pushed you to begin.

A phrase from your library
“What pushed me to start was...”
Source: podcast saved on Aug 12

Try once more
Start with the moment, then explain the product.
```

Avoid:

- exhaustive grammar-error lists;
- native-likeness scores;
- a fully rewritten script;
- many advanced expressions unrelated to the learner's existing language;
- inconsistent evaluation criteria between sessions.

### AI quality measures

- recommended-expression relevance rated at least 4/5 by 70% of users;
- recommended-expression acceptance or save rate at least 50%;
- target phrase retrieved without being shown in a later attempt by at least 40%;
- "this still sounds like me" rated at least 4/5 by 70%;
- two-attempt core-loop completion at least 50%;
- no automatic promotion from display or suggestion to `Retrieve` or `Use`.

These are validation gates, not efficacy claims.

## UX advantage for a solo creator

Large products can copy features. The initial advantage must come from focus,
speed, trust, and unusually low friction.

### Capture

- system share sheet;
- clipboard handoff;
- manual entry;
- optional screenshot OCR;
- source link and short user-selected context;
- no promise of bulk third-party transcript scraping or importing.

### Practice

- Topic widget: `Talk about My current project for 60 seconds`;
- one-tap entry into Mirror mode;
- live face view with no saved video;
- one compact analysis card;
- immediate second attempt;
- previous and current audio comparison.

### Review

Do not only ask whether the learner remembers a definition. Prompt the learner's
meaning:

```text
Phrase
“What I'm trying to do is...”

Prompt
Explain one thing you are currently building or changing.
```

### Initial product constraints

- English as the only target language;
- no full language curriculum;
- no AI avatar as the main interaction;
- no generic open-ended chatbot;
- no corporate admin product;
- no public social feed;
- no expansion to many target languages before the English analysis loop works.

## Global creator-led go-to-market

### Distribution principle

Target one behavior globally through a few reachable creator-language clusters.
Do not localize the product identity around each nationality.

Creator fit criteria:

- teaches intermediate plateau, active vocabulary, immersion, sentence mining,
  self-talk, or speaking activation;
- audience comments contain forms of `I understand but cannot speak`, `I save
  phrases but forget them`, or `I always use simple words`;
- creates in Chinese, Spanish, Russian, or another language where the founder can
  obtain permission or a warm introduction;
- is willing to demonstrate a real phrase-to-self-talk workflow rather than only
  place an app advertisement.

### Organic participation

When a relevant creator publishes a video:

1. watch the full content and understand the creator's method;
2. leave a useful, specific comment that extends the discussion;
3. disclose product affiliation if mentioning the product;
4. do not paste the same comment across channels;
5. request creator permission before links, giveaways, or recruitment;
6. invite a small number of qualified learners to test one real workflow.

The useful contribution comes before the product link.

### Creator collaboration format

Recommended challenge:

> **Save 3. Say 1.**
>
> Save three expressions from this week's content. Use one in a one-minute Topic
> without looking at it.

The creator supplies the learning method or content context. The product supplies
the activation loop. Avoid reproducing copyrighted transcripts; use creator-approved
short examples, source links, or user-entered phrases.

### Content themes

- why saved expressions disappear during speech;
- Anki has thousands of cards, but spontaneous speech uses the same safe words;
- the difference between recognizing and retrieving an expression;
- why self-talk is different from another AI conversation;
- one phrase moving from a video into a personal Topic;
- first attempt versus second attempt;
- how to build Topics instead of collecting random vocabulary;
- why Mirror mode shows the learner but saves only audio.

### Acquisition campaigns are narrower than the brand

```text
Core identity
Turn collected English into personally usable speech

Campaigns
- the next interview
- the next meetup introduction
- a project explanation
- an idea from a video
- a show the learner wants to discuss
- moving abroad
```

Deadlines create acquisition. Durable Topics and Speaking Memory create retention.

## Growth and validation metrics

### North Star

> Weekly completed activation loops: a saved or recommended expression is tested
> through self-talk, followed by a second attempt or later unprompted retrieval.

### Core funnel

```text
qualified creator/community visitor
→ landing signup
→ first Topic
→ first self-talk attempt
→ analysis viewed
→ second attempt
→ saved phrase retrieved later
→ new or existing Topic revisited within 7–30 days
```

### Initial validation gates

- 5 of the first 10 qualified users complete two attempts;
- 4 of 10 retrieve or use a saved expression in a later attempt;
- 4 of 10 return to an existing Topic or create another practice request;
- median capture-to-practice-ready time under 60 seconds initially, moving toward
  10–20 seconds as native capture surfaces are added;
- recommendation relevance at least 4/5 for 70% of evaluated recommendations;
- creator/community source recorded for every signup;
- compare activation and return behavior by creator cluster, not nationality.

Do not use total registrations, saved phrases, or AI messages as evidence of product
value on their own.

## Solo-creator strategic boundary

The product should not claim that large competitors are unable to build these
features. They can.

The initial solo advantage is:

- a narrower user and loop;
- faster qualitative iteration with intermediate learners;
- a founder-level relationship with creator communities;
- careful handling of personal meaning and voice;
- friction reductions too small to headline a large roadmap but large enough to
  change daily behavior;
- a consistent longitudinal model of Topic, phrase provenance, and actual retrieval.

The long-term asset is not a mirror, widget, or model call. It is the learner's
private graph of:

```text
meaning
↔ Topic
↔ expression and source
↔ attempt
↔ retrieval evidence
↔ future use
```

## Next brand task

The strategy should be locked before selecting a new public name. `Saylo` remains a
working name because another English-shadowing product is already using the same
name in app stores.

### Brand brief to carry into naming

The new name should feel:

- personal rather than corporate;
- expressive rather than corrective;
- active without sounding like a fitness or productivity tracker;
- globally pronounceable;
- compatible with Topics, Mirror, and Speaking Memory;
- broad enough for life, interests, study, projects, and work;
- credible for an adult intermediate learner;
- distinct from AI tutor, translation, accent, and workplace-coach brands.

### Naming territories to explore

1. **Own voice and expression**
   A name about making language feel like the learner's own.

2. **Return and activation**
   A name about saved language coming back at the moment of speech.

3. **Studio and rehearsal**
   A name about a private place to shape and practice speech.

4. **Memory and continuity**
   A name about Topics and language accumulating across time.

These are exploration territories, not candidate names.

### Naming constraints

Reject a candidate before preference testing if it:

- conflicts with an existing language, speech, voice, education, or AI product;
- lacks a practical domain or app-store identity path;
- is difficult to pronounce across the initial creator-language communities;
- strongly implies work-only, beginner-only, accent correction, or AI chat;
- depends on an English pun that does not travel globally;
- is visually or phonetically too close to `Saylo` or another existing brand.

### Recommended brand-sprint sequence

1. Freeze this document as the strategy brief.
2. Generate 20–30 names across the four territories.
3. Run knockout checks across app stores, web search, domains, social handles,
   USPTO, WIPO, and relevant international trademark databases.
4. Reduce to 5–8 viable candidates.
5. Test name pronunciation, unaided category association, emotional fit, and recall
   with 8–12 target users from multiple first-language communities.
6. Develop lightweight wordmark and hero-copy studies for the top three.
7. Select a working finalist, then obtain professional trademark advice before a
   public store launch or material marketing spend.

### Brand-test questions

- What kind of product do you imagine from this name?
- How would you pronounce it after seeing it once?
- Does it feel like a tutor, a work tool, a social app, or a private studio?
- Would it still fit a Topic about a film, a project, moving abroad, and a meetup?
- What do you remember about it ten minutes later?
