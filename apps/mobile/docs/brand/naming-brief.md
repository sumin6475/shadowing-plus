# Public Brand Naming Brief

> Status: Current input for candidate generation
>
> Updated: 2026-08-16

This brief translates the canonical product strategy into rules for selecting a new
public name. It does not clear or select a candidate. App-store, domain, social, and
trademark checks are required before public adoption.

## What the brand must hold

The product helps an intermediate or advanced English learner turn language they
notice into language they can use as themselves.

The learner:

1. saves useful English from content or daily life;
2. connects it to a personal Topic;
3. speaks freely through self-talk or Mirror mode;
4. receives one restrained, meaning-aware repair;
5. tries again and keeps evidence that the language returned.

The durable value is a private speaking memory: the learner's own Topics,
expressions, Attempts, and retrieval history accumulating over time.

## Audience

The first user already knows enough English to consume real content but still
experiences the gap between recognition and spontaneous speech. They learn
independently, collect expressions, and want to talk about their own life,
interests, ideas, study, projects, and work.

This is an English-first global product. Initial Chinese-, Spanish-, or
Russian-speaking creator communities are distribution clusters, not separate
product identities or nationality-based targets.

## Position

- **Consumer category:** personal speaking studio
- **Functional category:** personal language activation
- **Core method:** Topic-based self-talk
- **Consumer promise:** The English you save should come back when you speak.
- **Emotional movement:** from “I know this, but I cannot reach it” to “this is
  language I can actually use as myself.”

The name does not need to describe English learning literally. It must leave room
for personal life and culture as well as study, projects, and work.

## Brand character

The brand should feel:

- personal, private, and self-directed;
- expressive rather than corrective;
- adult, editorial, and quietly intelligent;
- warm enough for daily use without becoming playful or childish;
- active without resembling a fitness or productivity tracker;
- supportive without presenting AI as the author or teacher.

It should not feel like:

- an AI tutor or generic conversation bot;
- an accent-removal or pronunciation-scoring service;
- a corporate communication coach;
- a beginner curriculum or streak game;
- a public social-performance feed.

## Naming territories

### Primary: return + ownership

The strongest territory combines the product's functional difference—saved
language returning in speech—with the emotional result of making that language
one's own. It can express activation without sounding technical.

### Secondary: studio + rehearsal

A private place to shape, hear, and repeat one's speech. This territory fits the
experience but should support the brand rather than reduce the product to a single
practice room or recording feature.

### Supporting: memory + continuity

Topics and language accumulate across time. This supports retention and product
depth, but a name that feels passive, archival, or note-taking-only should fail.

### Supporting: voice + expression

The learner develops a usable personal voice. Avoid generic `voice`, `speak`, and
`say` constructions unless the combination is unusually ownable.

## Immediate rejection rules

Reject a candidate before preference testing if it:

- conflicts with an existing language, speech, voice, education, or AI product;
- lacks a credible app-store, domain, and social-identity path;
- is difficult to pronounce after one reading across several first-language groups;
- depends on an English pun or spelling trick that does not travel globally;
- strongly implies beginner lessons, work-only communication, accent correction,
  transcription, translation, or AI chat;
- is visually or phonetically close to `Saylo` or another established product;
- requires a long explanation to connect it to personal expression and use.

`Shadowing+` and `Saylo` are excluded as public-name candidates. They remain only as
technical or historical identifiers until migration.

## Candidate scorecard

Candidates that pass the rejection rules are scored out of 100:

| Criterion | Weight | Test |
|---|---:|---|
| Distinctiveness and clearance path | 25 | Search, stores, domains, handles, trademark databases |
| Global pronunciation | 20 | Read once by users from multiple first-language groups |
| Emotional fit | 15 | Feels personal, expressive, adult, and private |
| Product breadth | 15 | Fits film, travel, study, project, meetup, and work Topics |
| Ownability | 15 | Can support a recognizable wordmark, search result, and verbal identity |
| Recall | 10 | Remembered and spelled ten minutes after first exposure |

A high preference score cannot rescue a candidate that fails legal or marketplace
clearance. A descriptive name is not automatically clearer if it disappears among
other speaking apps.

## Voice anchor for name testing

Test every candidate in plain English, not only as a standalone word:

```text
[Name] is your personal speaking studio.
Save the English you notice. Make it yours by speaking.
The English you save should come back when you speak.
```

The surrounding copy should be calm and concrete. Use verbs such as `save`,
`connect`, `speak`, `try again`, `return`, and `make it yours`. Avoid inflated claims
such as `master fluency`, `speak like a native`, or `perfect your English`.

## Naming sprint deliverable

The next naming round should:

1. generate 24 candidates across the four territories;
2. record a one-line rationale and pronunciation for each;
3. run fast marketplace and collision knockouts before aesthetic ranking;
4. retain 5–8 viable candidates for multilingual user testing;
5. test the finalists in a wordmark, app-icon label, and hero-copy context;
6. seek professional trademark advice before public launch or material spend.
